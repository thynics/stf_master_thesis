\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter evaluates the proposed CUDASTF runtime scheduler as a sequence of
controlled experiments. The first part isolates the placement scheduler by
disabling DVFS and comparing \texttt{bandit\_scheduler} against HEFT. The
second part evaluates the \texttt{win\_dvfs} extension by comparing kernel
latency, kernel energy, and kernel power against a no-DVFS baseline. This
separation is intentional: placement changes the GPU selected for a task,
whereas DVFS changes the frequency level used after placement. Mixing the two
effects in the same headline metric would make the mechanism difficult to
interpret.

The main placement result is that \texttt{bandit\_scheduler} improves
geometric-mean kernel latency by \(7.146\%\) across six benchmarks when the
mean of each five-run batch is used. The strongest improvements occur on
\texttt{cg}, \texttt{cholesky}, and \texttt{lu\_decomposition}, and the
locality-attribution experiment shows that these wins coincide with large
reductions in copy traffic. The DVFS result is more modest but consistent:
\texttt{win\_dvfs} reduces geometric-mean kernel energy by \(2.904\%\) with a
geometric-mean kernel-latency overhead of \(0.626\%\). Four benchmarks show
clear kernel-energy reductions, one benchmark shows a smaller energy reduction
with a visible latency trade-off, and one benchmark remains near neutral.

\section{Experimental Protocol and Evidence Map}
\label{sec:results-protocol}

All final experiments target single-node, eight-GPU CUDASTF execution. The
application-visible GPU set is \(\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}\), and every row used in
the final tables sampled all eight GPUs. Each benchmark row is launched as a
fresh process so that runtime scheduler state, CUDA context state, and DVFS
state do not intentionally carry over between measured runs.

The evaluation uses six benchmarks: \texttt{fdtd}, \texttt{cg},
\texttt{miniweather}, \texttt{cholesky}, \texttt{lu\_decomposition}, and
\texttt{nbody\_blocked}. The placement experiments use HEFT and
\texttt{bandit\_scheduler} with DVFS disabled. The DVFS experiments use a
no-DVFS baseline and the \texttt{win\_dvfs} configuration. For DVFS, kernel
energy is the primary energy metric because it measures the energy integrated
over the benchmark kernel window. Whole-process energy is useful as auxiliary
context, but it is not used for the headline decisions in this chapter.

\section{E0: Baseline and Measurement Gate}
\label{sec:results-e0}

E0 is an infrastructure check rather than a final performance result. It
verifies that all six benchmarks run under both HEFT and
\texttt{bandit\_scheduler}, that kernel timestamps and NVML kernel-energy
measurements are present, and that all rows use the intended eight-GPU
configuration with DVFS disabled. This gate is important because later
experiments rely on kernel-window energy and kernel-window latency rather than
only process-level elapsed time.

The single E0 run already shows the qualitative shape expected from the
placement scheduler: \texttt{cg}, \texttt{cholesky}, and
\texttt{lu\_decomposition} improve substantially, while \texttt{miniweather}
and \texttt{nbody\_blocked} are almost unchanged. However, E0 uses only one
measured run per row, so the final placement conclusions are taken from E1,
E2, and E3.

\section{E1: Placement Performance}
\label{sec:results-e1}

E1 measures placement latency with DVFS disabled. The experiment is split into
three five-run batches so that no single job becomes too large: one batch for
\texttt{fdtd} and \texttt{miniweather}, one for \texttt{cg} and
\texttt{lu\_decomposition}, and one for \texttt{cholesky} and
\texttt{nbody\_blocked}. The comparison is HEFT versus
\texttt{bandit\_scheduler}. Because DVFS is disabled, any latency difference
in this section comes from placement and runtime scheduling behavior, not from
frequency changes.

Using the mean latency for each benchmark, \texttt{bandit\_scheduler} obtains
a six-benchmark geometric-mean speedup of \(1.076965\times\), equivalent to a
normalized runtime reduction of \(7.146\%\) relative to HEFT. Using medians,
the geometric-mean speedup is \(1.063759\times\), equivalent to a normalized
runtime reduction of \(5.994\%\). Table~\ref{tab:results-e1-placement}
summarizes the per-benchmark results.

The key observation is that the placement scheduler does not create a uniform
speedup across all workloads. It is effective when the task graph exposes
useful copy-saving placement alternatives, and it stays close to HEFT when
such alternatives are absent. This behavior is desirable: the scheduler is
designed as a HEFT-relative residual policy, not as an unconstrained learned
placement rule.

\section{E2: Locality Attribution}
\label{sec:results-e2}

E2 explains the E1 latency behavior by measuring copy traffic and non-HEFT
deviation rate. DVFS remains disabled. The experiment focuses on three
classes: strong locality winners, a modest or noisy case, and neutral
workloads. The central question is whether speedups correspond to measurable
locality improvements rather than accidental runtime variation.

Table~\ref{tab:results-e2-locality} shows that the largest latency
improvements align with copy-byte reductions. \texttt{cholesky} reduces median
latency by \(9.269\%\) while reducing copy bytes by \(38.220\%\), and
\texttt{lu\_decomposition} reduces median latency by \(15.891\%\) while
reducing copy bytes by \(23.905\%\). \texttt{fdtd} shows a smaller but still
consistent locality win. By contrast, \texttt{miniweather} and
\texttt{nbody\_blocked} have zero deviation rate and zero copy-byte reduction,
which explains their near-neutral latency behavior.

These results support the intended mechanism of the placement layer. When the
runtime observes a non-HEFT placement that can reduce copy traffic while
keeping the predicted finish-time penalty bounded, the residual gate accepts
the deviation. When no such opportunity exists, the scheduler naturally
collapses back to HEFT-like behavior. This explains both the strong wins and
the neutral controls without requiring a separate benchmark-specific policy.

\section{E4 and E5: Final DVFS Energy Results}
\label{sec:results-e4-e5}

E4 and E5 evaluate the final \texttt{win\_dvfs} configuration. Unlike E1--E3,
DVFS is enabled in the \texttt{win\_dvfs} rows. The comparison is against a
no-DVFS baseline, and the primary metric is kernel energy. The result should
therefore be read as an energy-control result, not as additional placement
evidence.

Table~\ref{tab:results-e5-dvfs} reports the final six-benchmark DVFS table.
\texttt{fdtd}, \texttt{miniweather}, \texttt{cg}, and

\texttt{nbody\_blocked} is near neutral, which is consistent with the

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:results-threats}

The placement results are measured on a single eight-GPU node and may vary with
GPU topology, interconnect bandwidth, CUDA driver behavior, and benchmark
scale. The evaluation uses several repeated runs for E1--E3, but some
benchmarks still show noise. \texttt{fdtd} is the clearest example: the mean
and median E1 results point in different directions because the per-run order
contains mixed outcomes. The thesis therefore treats \texttt{fdtd} as a
fragile or modest locality case rather than as a headline placement win.

The locality attribution uses runtime copy metrics as mechanism evidence.
Copy-byte reductions explain the observed wins well for
\texttt{cholesky} and \texttt{lu\_decomposition}, but they do not capture every
possible source of runtime variation, such as queueing effects, kernel launch
overheads, or overlap between computation and communication. The claim is
therefore mechanism-aligned rather than a complete causal decomposition of all
runtime effects.

The DVFS conclusion is based on kernel-window energy and kernel-window latency.
This is the right metric for the energy-control question studied here, but it
does not replace a full-system power study. The final DVFS rows show a
consistent aggregate energy reduction with small aggregate latency overhead,
yet larger run counts and additional hardware platforms would be needed before
claiming universal behavior. E7 also shows that diagnostic logging and compact
DVFS control are not free, so final timing and energy claims should rely on the
validation rows rather than on instrumentation-heavy diagnostic rows.

\section{Results Summary}
\label{sec:results-summary}

The experimental evidence supports both layers of the proposed design. For
placement, \texttt{bandit\_scheduler} improves geometric-mean kernel latency
by \(7.146\%\) using mean E1 latencies across six benchmarks, while the E2
locality experiment shows that the strongest wins coincide with large copy-byte
reductions. The neutral controls show the complementary behavior: when no
copy-saving opportunity is present, the scheduler stays close to HEFT rather
than forcing arbitrary deviations. E3 confirms that the selected default
configuration is the best cross-workload choice among the tested sensitivity
variants.

For DVFS, \texttt{win\_dvfs} reduces geometric-mean kernel energy by
\(2.904\%\) with \(0.626\%\) geometric-mean kernel-latency overhead. The
mechanism evidence shows that the strongest energy wins are associated with
lower kernel average power and, in most cases, visible exposure to lower SM
clock levels. The final result is therefore not a universal claim that every
benchmark benefits from DVFS. Instead, it is a controlled result showing that a
guarded proposal--commit DVFS layer can extract energy savings from several
CUDASTF workloads while keeping the aggregate latency cost small and leaving
low-opportunity workloads near the baseline.
